# English summaries of the fictional autobiographies (D01–D06)

These are one-paragraph summaries of fictional autobiographies written for the public demo. The people, companies and events do not exist. The summaries describe the content of each text only; they do not state the attribute values in `persons.csv`. The Japanese texts in `texts/` are the only inputs the engine uses; these summaries are for readers who do not read Japanese.

## D01

The narrator grows up near a small plating workshop run by their father, studies applied chemistry through a master's degree, and joins a surface-treatment chemicals maker as a researcher. After a decade in the central laboratory, including the development of a product that anticipates a regulatory change, the narrator becomes plant manager and builds an early-warning system with veteran operators, while repeatedly postponing pipe replacement. In the second year as president, a pipe failure leaks acid outside the plant; the main customer suspends business and the firm posts its first large loss. The narrator rebuilds over three years through public replacement schedules, safety disclosure and monthly visits to the customer, and reflects that technical strength rests on safety.

## D02

Raised in a family general store in a mountain town, the narrator studies literature, hopes to become an editor, and instead joins a regional food wholesaler as a salesperson, learning to listen to shopkeepers. When the firm opens its own supermarkets, the narrator runs the first store, rises to president, and is told to leave the numbers to the finance director. In the third year as president, the largest wholesale customer enters court-supervised reorganization, leaving uncollectable receivables and a looming cash shortfall. The narrator tells all employees the truth, negotiates with the bank, closes two stores, and accepts voluntary redundancies. Two years later the firm is stable, and the narrator has learned not to fear numbers.

## D03

A child who takes apart clocks and radios studies precision engineering, joins a measuring-instrument maker's development department, and spends fifteen years there, learning from one failed product that usability matters as much as performance. As design director, the narrator resists competing on price and instead leads a three-year project to build instruments that feed measurements back to the production line. As executive vice president, the narrator spends half a year abroad establishing an overseas plant, discovering that documented reasons, not just procedures, transfer precision. The presidency is calm and profitable; the narrator keeps research spending high, sends young engineers abroad, and visits twenty customer plants a year.

## D04

The grandchild of a hot-spring innkeeper studies sociology and joins a national hotel chain in human resources, first working a month in housekeeping and banquet service before redesigning new-employee training. Over ten years in HR, the narrator learns that people leave workplaces where they cannot learn or be recognized. As general manager of a regional hotel, the narrator greets guests each morning and institutes a monthly employee-proposal meeting. Back at headquarters, the narrator insists that new hotels be approved only when their key staff are identified. The ten-year presidency is uneventful and steady; the narrator visits every hotel annually without a guided tour and chooses a successor from HR.

## D05

The narrator's mother kept a nightly notebook of small daily things, and the narrator has kept one every day since university, recording names and offhand remarks rather than decisions. After student travels that taught the value of asking for help, the narrator joins a household-goods trading company because the interviewer listened to the end. Early on, the narrator memorizes hundreds of business partners' names and later wins over a difficult client by writing weekly letters without any request in them. Two mentors shape the narrator: a first boss who always asked "so what will you do?" and a calligraphy teacher who noticed haste. The text closes on morning habits, listening before deciding, and the nature of personal connections.

## D06

The narrator's grandfather, a farmer of few words, summarized each day in one sentence on the walk home, a habit the narrator keeps as president. A taciturn father once said, "Never look down on anyone's work," a sentence the narrator carries throughout a career at a household-goods manufacturer. A talkative first boss taught three simple rules: look people in the eye, arrive ten minutes early, ask when you do not understand. Rising quietly to president, the narrator makes three promises to employees and keeps a box on the desk for proposals, answering each within a week. The narrator's way of working is summarized as reading, walking, and waiting, and the text ends by passing the father's sentence on to younger people.
